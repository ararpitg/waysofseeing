Ways of Seeing · Counter-Mapping

Indigenous Counter-Mapping: Who Draws the Land in Jharkhand?

Maps, documentary film, and Adivasi claims to ground, told as a story in three readings and three maps.

If the state maps Adivasi land in order to turn it into data, parcels to be listed, leased, and auctioned, how might a counter-map make Indigenous spatial knowledge visible without exposing it to the same gaze?

That question began as a fork from my dissertation. The dissertation studies how Adivasi (Indigenous) media collectives in Jharkhand, India, make films and keep records against the categories the state uses to describe them. The fork moved from the cinematic frame to the spatial database: from studying Adivasi media to asking what kind of map it might need.

The page that follows is built in two movements. The first is a reading of the scholarship, in three short chapters: what critical cartographers have said a map does, how those arguments land on the Adivasi question in India, and why a map of Adivasi media, rather than of Adivasi land, is the counter-map worth drawing. The second is three maps, each built to see differently, and each with its own way of being read. You scroll through each map with a guide, and at the end of each you take the controls.

One rule holds throughout. The second and third maps never go finer than a district. The first map can zoom to a rooftop, and the reason it is allowed to is part of the argument.

Chapter 1 · The basics

What a Map Does

Most of us learned maps as windows: the better the map, the clearer the glass. Critical cartography begins by giving that idea a history, and then by asking who built the window and what it was built to show.

  1. 1953–1995

    The map as a clear signal Arthur H. Robinson

    After the Second World War, Robinson remade American academic cartography as a science of design. His aim was what he called map effectiveness: a map succeeds when its reader receives, without noise, the information the cartographer meant to send (Kitchin and Dodge 2007, citing Robinson et al. 1995). Matthew Edney shows how Robinson used the history of cartography itself to define what proper academic cartography should be after 1945 (Edney 2005). In this model the map is a channel, and every problem is a technical one.

  2. 1988–1989

    The map as power J. B. Harley

    Harley asked what the clear channel leaves out. He proposed reading maps as “thick” texts, a socially constructed form of knowledge whose silences are as deliberate as its lines. The maps of states, he argued, carry the state’s interests most effectively when they look most neutral (Harley 1988; 1989). For Harley the measured map is not a lie, but it is never only a picture of the land: it is also an instrument for holding it.

  3. 1992–2010

    The mask Denis Wood

    Wood pushed further. Cartographers, he argued, have pretended that a map’s authority comes from its resemblance to the world; in fact it comes from its mask of detached neutrality, an authority it takes from the institution that made it and then hides (Wood 1993a). Maps work by serving interests, and the work is done best when no one sees it being done (Wood 1992; Wood, Fels, and Krygier 2010). Wood also faulted Harley for never letting go of the map as a representation of reality, a picture rather than a proposition (Wood 1993b).

  4. 1995

    Counter-mapping Nancy Lee Peluso

    In Kalimantan, Indonesia, forest communities drew sketch maps of territory they claimed by custom, using the state’s own techniques to assert rights the official forest map ignored or criminalized. Peluso named the practice counter-mapping (Peluso 1995). She also named its risk. A counter-map wins by speaking the state’s cartographic language, so its gains are recorded in the terms of the apparatus it contests. A claim that can be drawn on a map can be answered on a map.

  5. 1997 & 2010

    Mapping India Matthew Edney · Sumathi Ramaswamy

    For India the argument has its own literature. Edney shows that the British surveys of 1765–1843 did not simply record India; they constructed it as a single, knowable, governable space, so that the empire and its map were made together (Edney 1997). Ramaswamy follows the nation into a different kind of map: Mother India, the goddess drawn onto the outline of the territory, a “barefoot cartography” of popular prints that made the map an object of devotion (Ramaswamy 2010). Between them, the subcontinent’s maps are shown to be acts of rule and acts of feeling, and rarely neutral either way.

  6. 2006

    Critical cartography Jeremy Crampton · John Krygier

    Crampton and Krygier define critical cartography as a “one-two punch”: new mapping practices joined to theoretical critique, linking geographic knowledge to power and therefore to politics (Crampton and Krygier 2006). They note that mapping has been slipping out of the hands of the elites who long controlled it, the great map houses, the state, and the academy, into counter-mappings, participatory GIS, artists, and anyone with open-source tools. The map you are about to use is one small instance of that slippage.

  7. 2007

    The map as practice Rob Kitchin · Martin Dodge

    Kitchin and Dodge propose that a map is not a finished object but something brought into being each time it is used: ontogenetic rather than ontic, always a mapping rather than a map (Kitchin and Dodge 2007). If maps are made in use, then who uses a map, for what task, and with what tools, is part of what the map is. A cadastral sheet read by a revenue officer and the same sheet read by a village elder are, in this sense, two different maps.

  8. 1998–2022

    Beyond the grid Performance cartography · Indigenous visions

    Not every map is drawn. Woodward and Lewis describe performance cartography: chant, dance, procession, place name, or story whose purpose is to hold spatial knowledge (Woodward and Lewis 1998). Kalani Akana reads Kaua‘i this way, through the chants, sayings, and place names that mapped the island long before Cook’s charts (Akana 2013). The Zuni map art project painted homelands as story and relation rather than measured distance (Enote and McLerran 2011). Nour Joudah’s work on Palestine and Hawai‘i reads Indigenous mapping as a way of picturing decolonized futures, and of holding time as continuity rather than as a story of loss (Joudah 2022).

The question this page asks, then, is no longer whether a map is accurate. It is whose map, made in use by whom, and built to see what.

Chapter 2 · India and Jharkhand

The Adivasi Question

The Indian constitution already maps Indigeneity twice: once as a list of people, once as a map of land. The two do not always line up, and much of Adivasi politics happens in the gap between them.

Two instruments: a list and a map

Adivasi, “first dwellers,” is the self-name many Indigenous communities in India use. The state’s word is Scheduled Tribe. In the 2011 Census about 104 million people, 8.6 per cent of India, were counted as Scheduled Tribes. Recognition comes through Article 342 of the constitution, which lists tribes state by state, so that a community is a Scheduled Tribe in relation to a named state and may not be one in the next. Protection comes through Article 244 and the Fifth Schedule, which attach special administration to land: Scheduled Areas in ten states, Jharkhand among them, where the Governor holds special legislative powers and a Tribes Advisory Council advises on tribal welfare.

One instrument indexes people, the other indexes ground. A family can carry its listing and leave its ground, as the Oraon and Munda migrants settled in the Andaman Islands did, and find that neither instrument follows them. My dissertation reads Akhra’s films against exactly these two instruments.

A paper trail on the plateau

On the Chotanagpur plateau the map arrived with revenue. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 tied land to tax through intermediary landlords. Biju Toppo of Akhra put the consequence plainly when we spoke in October 2026: before the Settlement a village had no use for a map, because land was the village’s and the question of whose it was did not arise in a form a document could answer; afterwards, the record became necessary at the very moment it became dangerous (Toppo 2026). In 1832 colonial officials marked the Damin-i-koh in the Rajmahal hills with masonry pillars, and the debt and dispossession that followed fed the Santal Hul of 1855 (Andersen 2022). Later law recorded, and so protected, some customary tenures: the Chota Nagpur Tenancy Act of 1908 defines the Mundari khuntkatti right of those who first cleared the forest.

The survey sheets those laws rest on are still in use. When a land dispute arises today, the old settlement map and the revisional map are read side by side by an amin, a surveyor, and the village often asks first for the elder whose boundaries the neighbours recognize. Counter-cartography at its most ordinary runs inside the state’s own idiom; what the village supplies is the person it trusts to read it.

Counter-mapping as law, and as stone

Jharkhand was carved from Bihar on 15 November 2000, after a movement that ran for most of the century. Two laws gave counter-mapping a legal form. The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act of 1996 gave the gram sabha, the village assembly, powers over its commons and a say before its land is acquired. The Forest Rights Act of 2006 made a version of Peluso’s practice into procedure: to claim community forest resource rights, gram sabhas map their customary boundaries with elders and neighbouring villages, often with GPS (Zumbish and Jha 2022).

The state kept datafying. In 2016 Jharkhand’s Land Bank listed about 2.1 million acres of “government” land for investors, nearly half of it forest, taking in village commons, sarna groves, and khuntkatti land (Hassan 2024; Sushmita 2022). Villages answered in stone. From 2017, in Khunti, they raised pathalgadi slabs at their entrances, drawing on the custom of memorial stones and inscribing them with Fifth Schedule provisions and claims of gram sabha self-rule. More than ten thousand people were named in sedition cases; in December 2019 a new state government decided to withdraw them (Sharma 2019; Scroll Staff 2019). A slab at a village gate is a map in Kitchin and Dodge’s sense: made in use, for a task, and read differently by the village and the police.

Alpa Shah’s ethnography of Jharkhand adds a caution any counter-map here has to carry. Rights campaigns built on a romantic image of the Adivasi, she shows, can end up serving those who speak for Adivasis more than the poorest Adivasis themselves (Shah 2010). A counter-map is also a claim to speak for someone.

Chapter 3 · Media, mapping, and counter-storytelling

Why Counter-Map Adivasi Media?

The camera and the survey grew up together. Both were instruments for looking at Adivasi people and land from outside. Indigenous media turned one of them around. A counter-map of that media puts both reversals in the same frame.

Two instruments of the outside view

Robert Flaherty’s Nanook of the North (1922) fixed the Indigenous subject in front of the lens and the metropolitan audience behind it, and visual anthropology took that arrangement as its founding grammar. The survey did the same to land: it fixed the plateau on a sheet that someone else would read. Faye Ginsburg’s early work asked whether the camera, taken up by Indigenous makers, would erode their cultures or sustain them, and answered that such work had to be judged by how it was embedded in community relations: who made it, with whose permission, and for whom (Ginsburg 1991; 1994). Michelle Raheja calls the result visual sovereignty, Indigenous makers taking up the conventions that once described them and revising them from inside (Raheja 2007).

A film is a map that moves

Film theory has long treated cinema as cartographic. Tom Conley reads films as maps that locate and orient their viewers (Conley 2007); Giuliana Bruno writes of film as a haptic geography, a way of moving through space rather than looking down on it (Bruno 2002). Akhra’s films are spatial in a more literal sense. Gadi Lohardaga Mail rides the narrow-gauge line from Ranchi to Lohardaga; Loha Garam Hai walks the sponge-iron belt; Kora Rajee and Tapu Rajee follow migrants to the tea gardens and the Andamans. In my dissertation I read these as spatial acts: a film can hold a population in view for an hour without resolving what to call it, which the list and the map cannot do.

Counter-storytelling

Richard Delgado argued that groups pushed to the margins tell stories to unsettle the account the majority tells about them, and that those stories are a form of argument, not a substitute for one (Delgado 1989). Daniel Solórzano and Tara Yosso made counter-storytelling a research method: telling the stories of people whose experiences are seldom told, in order to expose and challenge the stories that pass as common sense (Solórzano and Yosso 2002). Indigenous scholars place storytelling at the centre of their own research practice (Smith 1999; Archibald 2008). In Jharkhand the counter-story is told in Kurukh, Mundari, Nagpuri, and Santali, sung on a passenger train, and projected on a sheet in a village at night. Mapping it is a way of asking where those stories were told, about what ground, and for whom.

Who paid for the camera

Documentary in India was born funded. The state’s Films Division, public broadcasters, development organisations such as PRADAN, the Tribal Research Institute in Ranchi, and corporate foundations such as Tata Steel’s have all paid for Adivasi films, commissioned them, or screened them. My dissertation calls the position these funders offer the beneficiary slot: a place from which Adivasi makers are invited to speak, on terms set elsewhere. The films do not simply obey those terms. Akhra won a Films Division grant for Naachi Se Baanchi and made a film of Adivasi autonomy the state never intended to underwrite. So the second map below shows not only where the camera went, but who sent it, and where the films are now seen.

Why not map the villages?

Because the impulse to plot Indigenous knowledge onto a digital map can itself expose communities to the surveillance a counter-map is meant to resist. The unit of the maps that follow is the film, not the village; the screening, not the grove. And because Donna Haraway’s name for the view from above, the “god trick” of seeing everything from nowhere (Haraway 1988), is exactly what a satellite basemap offers, the first map hands you that trick on purpose, so that you can feel what the other two decline.

A country with no lines

This is India as the Sentinel-2 satellites saw it in 2024, stitched into a cloudless mosaic. Notice what is missing. There are no borders, no state lines, no names. Every boundary you have seen on a map of India was drawn onto an image like this one by someone, for some purpose.

2 of 5

Jharkhand, drawn in

Now one set of lines: the twenty-four districts of Jharkhand, from the Census of 2011. The state was carved from Bihar in November 2000. Its forested plateau holds a large share of India’s coal, iron ore, and bauxite, and about a quarter of its people are counted as Scheduled Tribes. Hover a district for its name.

3 of 5

The bauxite plateau

Lohardaga, Gumla, and Latehar sit on a laterite plateau rich in bauxite, the ore of aluminium. Between 2021 and 2024 the Ministry of Mines published five bauxite exploration blocks here. At one, Madua Pat-I, the exploration report counts 1.397 million tonnes of inferred ore and, under “Demographic Data of the Block,” twenty-four families and 115 people whose only livelihood is farming.

The satellite is built to see the first figure. The rest of this page keeps the second in view.

4 of 5

Jharia, closer

Closer still: the Jharia coalfield in Dhanbad, among the oldest coal mining districts in India and the largest coalfield in the state by proved reserves. The grey scars are open-cast pits. At this scale the satellite sees every pit and every road. It does not see who lives at their edge, what the ground was called before it was a coalfield, or what the people there have said about it.

Map 2 · Adivasi media

Where the Camera Went

Thirty-seven documentaries from this collection, placed at the district their record names, coloured by what they are about. Hover any point for its story; click for the film.

1 of 7

The whole collection

Nineteen films sit in Jharkhand’s districts, and ten reach beyond them, into Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Bengal, Assam, and the Andaman Islands. The makers are Akhra in Ranchi, Shriprakash and the Kritika group, Ektara in Bhopal, PRADAN, and my own small body of work. Each colour is a theme. A hollow point means the district is my reading of the film and still to be confirmed.

The map stops at district scale: you cannot zoom further.

2 of 7

Following the mines

The largest cluster is about extraction. Shriprakash’s Buddha Weeps in Jadugoda (1999) and Jadugoda: The Black Magic follow uranium mining and its tailings in East Singhbhum. Akhra’s Cheenti Ladi Haathi Se stages the fight against bauxite mining as an ant against an elephant; Loha Garam Hai lives beside the sponge-iron furnaces; Accumulated Injustice crosses into Odisha to the Rourkela steel plant. The camera went where the ore was taken out.

3 of 7

Dams, displacement, and self-rule

A second cluster follows water and law. Addo Miyad Ulgulan (1995) records the refusal to let the Koel and Karo rivers be dammed. Ek Hadsa Aur Bhi documents the Kutku-Mandal dam, whose closed sluice gate flooded some thirty-two villages in Palamu. Development Flows from the Barrel of the Gun (2003) joins Koel Karo to Kashipur in Odisha and Nagarnar in Chhattisgarh. Gram Sabha ki Kahani shows the village assemblies that PESA empowered over their commons.

4 of 7

The films that resist a pin

Films about song, festival, and memory are the hardest to pin. Karama and Sohrai follow festivals celebrated across the plateau, and Srishtikatha animates Munda creation stories; none names a district. Naachi Se Baanchi, on Ram Dayal Munda’s life, sits provisionally in Ranchi. These films belong to a calendar more than to a coordinate. The list below names every film not yet on the map, because leaving them off is itself a finding.

    5 of 7

    The people the map cannot hold

    Two films leave Jharkhand with the people who left it. Kora Rajee (2005) follows migrants to the tea gardens of North Bengal and Assam. Tapu Rajee follows Oraon and Munda migrants settled in the Andaman Islands, who have pressed since 1974 to be listed as Scheduled Tribes in relation to Jharkhand. Off the protected territory and absent from the list of the place they live, they fall outside both instruments of Chapter 2. The dashed lines are the only way this map can draw them.

    6 of 7

    Who paid for the camera

    Now the colours change from theme to funder, and the squares mark institutions. The Tribal Research Institute in Ranchi commissioned Karama, Sohrai, and Tapu Rajee and began its own Dharti Aaba Tribal Film Festival in 2025. Tata Steel Foundation in Jamshedpur co-produced Srishtikatha and has run the Samuday Ke Saath film programme within its Samvaad conclave since 2015. PRADAN commissioned Ek Ropa Dhaan and keeps a public repository of development films.     Closing

    What These Maps Refuse

    No village, grove, burial ground, boundary, or person is located on the second or third map, and none will be without the community’s decision. Their floor is the district, and every figure on them is one the state or a public body already publishes, or a place a film’s own record names. Where I have placed a film by my own reading, it is drawn hollow and labelled provisional until I can confirm it from the film. The first map is the exception, and it is there to make the rule visible.

    What comes next is slower work: sound for each region of the film map, chosen and cleared with Akhra, so that the map is heard as well as read, and kept from what Dylan Robinson calls hungry listening (Robinson 2020); and, with the village’s consent, the two cadastral sheets of one Jharkhand village read side by side the way an amin reads them. The same commitments govern the collection’s First Principles.
